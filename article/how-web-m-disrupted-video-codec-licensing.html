How WebM Disrupted Video Codec Licensing

The launch of Google’s open-source WebM project in 2010 fundamentally reshaped the digital media landscape by introducing a viable, royalty-free alternative to proprietary video codecs. At a time when patent pools were preparing to monetize web video streaming, WebM provided the leverage necessary to force competing standards—most notably H.264—to relax their restrictive licensing terms. This shift not only preserved free video distribution on the open web, but it also permanently altered how future video standards would be developed and commercialized.

The Looming Threat of Streaming Royalties

Prior to 2010, the video codec landscape was dominated by H.264 (AVC), a standard managed by the MPEG LA licensing consortium. While H.264 offered superior compression and broad hardware support, its licensing model carried significant uncertainty for web developers and content distributors.

MPEG LA had agreed to waive royalty fees for free, ad-supported internet video broadcasts, but only on a temporary basis scheduled to expire at the end of 2015. Browser vendors, streaming platforms, and independent publishers faced the prospect of paying substantial annual fees merely for streaming video over the internet.

Google’s Strategic Intervention

In May 2010, Google addressed this threat by acquiring On2 Technologies and releasing its VP8 codec under a royalty-free, BSD-style open-source license, packaging it within the new WebM container format. Supported out of the box by Mozilla Firefox, Opera, and Google Chrome, WebM demonstrated that high-definition web video could function without proprietary patent entanglements.

By backing WebM, major technology companies sent an unmistakable signal: the web would not accept a single, proprietary video standard if a viable royalty-free option existed.

MPEG LA’s Concession on H.264

The reaction from the proprietary camp was swift. Just three months after the launch of WebM, in August 2010, MPEG LA announced that it would permanently extend the royalty fee waiver for free internet broadcast video.

This decision was a direct defensive response to the WebM announcement. Had MPEG LA maintained its plan to levy royalties after 2015, content distributors and web platforms would have had an overwhelming financial incentive to abandon H.264 entirely in favor of WebM. Google's release effectively neutralized MPEG LA's leverage over free web streaming.

The Backlash Against HEVC/H.265

While H.264 licensing became more accommodating for internet distribution, the industry did not abandon aggressive licensing structures permanently. When the next-generation codec, High Efficiency Video Coding (HEVC/H.265), emerged, its patent pool fragmented among multiple competing organizations, including MPEG LA, HEVC Advance, and Velos Media.

These pools demanded steep royalties, annual caps running into millions of dollars, and even attempted to extract fees based on content distributor revenue. The existence and precedent of WebM gave the tech industry the confidence to resist HEVC's demands. Major web browsers and streaming platforms refused to adopt HEVC widely for general web use, leaving the codec largely confined to closed ecosystems like consumer electronics, broadcast television, and mobile hardware.

The Road to AV1 and Modern Royalty-Free Standards

WebM did more than just force concessions from H.264; it proved that an open-source model could successfully compete at scale. Google continued this trajectory by developing VP9 as part of the WebM project, deploying it across YouTube to slash bandwidth costs without paying licensing fees.

The success of VP8 and VP9 eventually led to the creation of the Alliance for Open Media (AOMedia) in 2015. Formed by Google, Mozilla, Cisco, Amazon, Netflix, Intel, and Microsoft, the consortium pooled their intellectual property to develop AV1—a state-of-the-art, open, royalty-free video standard.

By challenging the proprietary status quo in 2010, WebM established a precedent that remains central to modern digital architecture: the fundamental infrastructure of the public web must remain open, royalty-free, and accessible to everyone.
