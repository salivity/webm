Patent Pools That Challenged WebM Royalty-Free Status

When Google launched the WebM project in 2010, it aimed to provide an open, high-quality, and royalty-free video format for the web, centered around the VP8 and VP9 codecs. However, the format immediately drew scrutiny from established digital video licensing entities that argued modern video compression could not exist without infringing on existing intellectual property. The primary patent pools that sought to challenge WebM’s royalty-free status were organized by MPEG LA and, in later years, the licensing administrator Sisvel.

The MPEG LA VP8 Pool

Shortly after WebM was released, MPEG LA—the licensing administrator managing essential patent pools for standards like MPEG-2 and H.264—sought to dismantle WebM’s royalty-free claim. In February 2011, MPEG LA issued a formal call for patents claimed to be essential to VP8, the core video codec inside the original WebM container.

By July 2011, MPEG LA announced that twelve patent holders had stepped forward to create a joint VP8 patent pool. This move was intended to force WebM users and developers to pay licensing royalties, directly undermining Google’s promise of a cost-free web standard.

The standoff ended in March 2013, when Google reached a landmark agreement with MPEG LA and eleven licensing companies. Under the deal, Google acquired licenses for the disputed patents and negotiated the right to sublicense them to any VP8 user or developer worldwide at no cost. This agreement effectively neutralized MPEG LA's VP8 pool and preserved the royalty-free status of the first-generation WebM standard.

Sisvel's VP9 Patent Pool

The patent challenges to the WebM ecosystem resurfaced in March 2019 when Sisvel, an independent patent management company, launched a new patent pool targeting VP9—the successor codec used in WebM containers alongside VP8. Sisvel claimed that VP9, as well as the related AV1 codec, utilized proprietary technologies owned by various telecommunications and electronics firms, including Dolby, Philips, and Toshiba.

Unlike the earlier MPEG LA dispute, Sisvel did not secure a sweeping settlement with Google. Instead, Sisvel established a public licensing structure that required hardware and software implementers to pay per-unit royalties on devices capable of decoding or encoding VP9 video.

Google and the Alliance for Open Media (AOMedia) maintained that VP9 was developed independently and remained free of valid third-party patent claims. While Sisvel's pool created ongoing legal uncertainty and risk for commercial hardware manufacturers implementing WebM, the codec has continued to function as an open and royalty-free format across modern web browsers and open-source software platforms.
