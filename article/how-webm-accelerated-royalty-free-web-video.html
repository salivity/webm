How WebM Accelerated Royalty-Free Web Video

When Google launched the WebM project in 2010, the web was on the brink of being locked into proprietary, patent-encumbered video standards. By releasing the VP8 codec under an open-source, royalty-free license, Google disrupted the established licensing models dominated by the MPEG Licensing Authority (MPEG LA). This competitive shock wave not only forced patent pools to soften their terms for the web, but it also directly catalyzed the industry-wide coalition that created AV1, cementing royalty-free video as an open web standard.

The HTML5 Video Standoff

The arrival of the HTML5 <video> element aimed to make video a first-class citizen of the web without requiring third-party plugins like Adobe Flash. However, the standard stalled due to a fundamental disagreement over codecs:

  • Apple and Microsoft supported H.264, an efficient and hardware-accelerated standard, but one tightly controlled by the MPEG LA patent pool.
  • Mozilla and Opera refused to support H.264 natively, arguing that patent licensing fees made it impossible for independent developers and free software to implement the web standard. Instead, they backed Ogg Theora, which was royalty-free but technologically inferior.

Because H.264 carried future royalty uncertainties for free web content, developers faced potential fees simply for distributing video online.

WebM's Market Disruption

In May 2010, Google bought On2 Technologies, open-sourced its flagship VP8 codec, and launched WebM. Paired with the open Vorbis (and later Opus) audio format in a Matroska container, WebM provided a viable, high-quality alternative to H.264.

Google immediately baked WebM into Chrome and earned instant adoption from Mozilla Firefox and Opera. Because YouTube began encoding its massive catalog in WebM, the format instantly commanded significant web traffic. For the first time, proprietary licensing cartels faced a credible, well-funded competitor offering a performant, zero-cost alternative.

Immediate Concessions from MPEG LA

The competitive pressure yielded immediate results. Just months after WebM’s announcement, in August 2010, the MPEG LA announced that it would never charge royalties for free internet broadcast video encoded in H.264.

Prior to WebM, the MPEG LA had only guaranteed royalty exemptions in five-year blocks, leaving content creators, web platforms, and streaming services exposed to future licensing demands. The threat of browser vendors and platforms migrating entirely to WebM forced the MPEG LA to remove that threat permanently.

The Failure of HEVC and the Rise of VP9

The battle repeated itself with the next generation of video compression. The successor to H.264, HEVC (H.265), became mired in an unprecedented licensing disaster, with multiple competing patent pools demanding onerous royalties, caps, and even cuts of content distribution revenue.

Google responded by releasing VP9 as part of the WebM project. Unlike HEVC, VP9 was freely available and supported by modern browsers. Google used VP9 to stream 4K content on YouTube, proving that an open, royalty-free codec could match or exceed the technical efficiency of the most advanced proprietary formats.

The Formation of the Alliance for Open Media (AOMedia)

The proven success of WebM and VP9 demonstrated that open codecs were viable at global scale, but single-vendor stewardship remained a barrier to universal adoption. Hardware manufacturers and competing tech giants were hesitant to rely solely on a Google-controlled technology.

Recognizing this, Google joined forces with Mozilla, Cisco, Amazon, Netflix, Intel, and Microsoft in 2015 to form the Alliance for Open Media (AOMedia). By combining Google’s VP9 and VP10 development, Mozilla's Daala, and Cisco's Thor, the alliance created AV1.

WebM shifted the economics of web video from a closed tollbooth model to an open foundation. By providing a credible alternative when the web needed it most, WebM broke the monopoly of patent pools and guaranteed that the future of digital video remains open, unencumbered, and royalty-free.
