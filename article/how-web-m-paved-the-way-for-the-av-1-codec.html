How WebM Paved the Way for the AV1 Codec

The WebM project fundamentally reshaped internet video by proving that an open-source, royalty-free video format could compete with dominant proprietary standards. When Google open-sourced the VP8 codec and launched WebM in 2010, it initiated a continuous line of technical and architectural advancements that culminated in VP9. This technological lineage, combined with the legal frameworks established to protect open video delivery, served as the primary blueprint and code baseline for the Alliance for Open Media (AOMedia) when creating the AV1 codec.

The Origin of WebM and the Royalty-Free Mandate

Prior to WebM, the web relied heavily on patented codecs like H.264, which required complex licensing agreements managed by patent pools like MPEG LA. Following its acquisition of On2 Technologies, Google released the VP8 codec under a royalty-free BSD license and introduced the WebM container—a profile of the open Matroska format.

WebM demonstrated to the industry that royalty-free video compression could be deployed at global scale, particularly through integration in web browsers and YouTube. More importantly, it established the technical mechanisms required to innovate around entrenched video patents, creating a legally vetted path for open-source media algorithms.

Technical Evolution Through VP9

While VP8 established the ecosystem, VP9 provided the direct algorithmic foundation for next-generation compression. Developed under the WebM umbrella and released in 2013, VP9 was designed to rival the efficiency of HEVC (H.265). It introduced several key architectural innovations that separated it from earlier codecs:

  • Flexible Partitioning: VP9 replaced static 16x16 macroblocks with a hierarchical superblock structure (up to 64x64) that could be recursively split down to 4x4 partitions. This allowed the encoder to adapt efficiently to both smooth backgrounds and high-detail areas.
  • Expanded Prediction Modes: It expanded intra-frame prediction to 10 distinct directional modes, allowing much more accurate spatial prediction.
  • Asymmetric Discrete Cosine Transforms (DCT): VP9 introduced non-square transform blocks (such as 32x8 or 8x32) alongside standard square transforms, greatly improving compression efficiency for directional edges.
  • Advanced Reference Frames: It allowed frames to reference up to three different past frames (the last frame, a golden frame, and an alternative reference frame), significantly improving temporal prediction.

VP9 achieved approximately 50% better compression efficiency than VP8, proving that an open codec could deliver 4K streaming efficiently at scale without proprietary licensing overhead.

From VP10 to the Formation of AOMedia

By 2015, the video industry faced severe fragmentation due to the complicated, multi-pool licensing structure of HEVC. Google had already begun development on the next iteration of WebM, dubbed VP10. Simultaneously, other organizations were developing independent, open-source alternatives: Mozilla and the Xiph.Org Foundation were working on the Daala project, while Cisco was developing Thor to support video conferencing.

Recognizing that competing open-source standards would split market adoption, these companies—along with Amazon, Netflix, Intel, and Microsoft—formed the Alliance for Open Media in September 2015. Google contributed the unfinished VP10 codebase as the foundational starting point for the new unified project: AV1.

Integrating WebM Innovations into AV1

AV1 was built directly on top of the VP10 codebase, absorbing technical contributions from Daala and Thor along the way. Several core components of AV1 directly reflect their WebM heritage:

  • Superblock Architecture: AV1 expanded VP9's superblock design, scaling the concept up to 128x128 blocks with a recursive quadtree-plus-split partitioning structure, directly evolving VP9’s tree-based partitioning logic.
  • Directional Intra-Prediction: AV1 took the directional prediction concepts refined in VP8 and VP9 and expanded them to 56 directional angles, complete with gradient smoothing and cross-component prediction.
  • Compound Reference Systems: The multi-reference frame system built for VP9 was expanded in AV1 to support up to seven reference frames, enabling advanced compound prediction modes.
  • Loop Restoration and Filtering: The in-loop deblocking filter originally refined in VP8 and VP9 remained fundamental, complemented by AV1's Constrained Directional Enhancement Filter (CDEF) and loop restoration stages.

By supplying a mature, field-tested codebase in VP9/VP10, the WebM project significantly shortened the development cycle for AV1. It provided a reliable platform that allowed the working group to focus on adding cutting-edge coding tools rather than building a modern video codec from scratch.
