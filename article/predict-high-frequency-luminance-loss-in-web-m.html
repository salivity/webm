Predict High-Frequency Luminance Loss in WebM

Predicting high-frequency luminance loss during WebM compression requires mathematical models that evaluate how fine spatial details and sharp edges degrade under transform coding and quantization. WebM codecs—primarily VP8, VP9, and AV1—compress video by mapping pixel blocks into the frequency domain and discarding subtle visual data. Engineers and quality assessment pipelines rely on specific algorithmic frameworks, including frequency-domain energy metrics, Contrast Sensitivity Function (CSF) weighting, Visual Information Fidelity (VIF), and Lagrangian rate-distortion models, to calculate and forecast this loss before or during encoding.

Transform-Domain Energy and Quantization Modeling

WebM codecs split luminance (\(Y\)) channels into discrete blocks and apply spatial transforms, such as the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) or the Asymmetric Discrete Sine Transform (ADST). High-frequency luminance information resides in the higher-order transform coefficients (\(C_{u,v}\) where spatial frequencies \(u\) and \(v\) are large).

The most direct algorithm for predicting loss calculates the spectral energy attenuation caused by uniform or non-uniform scalar quantization:

\[\Delta E_{HF} = \sum_{u+v > T} \left( |C_{u,v}| - Q(C_{u,v}, q) \right)^2\]

Where:

  • \(C_{u,v}\) represents the pre-quantization transform coefficient.
  • \(Q(C_{u,v}, q) = \text{round}\left(\frac{C_{u,v}}{\Delta(q)}\right) \cdot \Delta(q)\) denotes the quantization function governed by the quantization parameter \(q\).
  • \(T\) is the spatial frequency threshold separating low-frequency structural components from high-frequency fine details.

When the step size \(\Delta(q)\) exceeds \(2 \cdot |C_{u,v}|\), the coefficient truncates to zero, resulting in complete high-frequency luminance elimination.

PSNR-HVS and Contrast Sensitivity Function (CSF) Algorithms

Standard Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) treats all errors uniformly across frequencies, making it insufficient for predicting perceived detail loss. The PSNR-HVS and PSNR-HVS-M algorithms address this by weighting errors with the human visual system's Contrast Sensitivity Function (CSF), such as the Mannos-Sakrison model:

\[A(f) = 2.6 \cdot (0.0192 + 0.114f) \cdot e^{-(0.114f)^{1.1}}\]

Where \(f\) represents the radial spatial frequency in cycles per degree of visual angle.

PSNR-HVS applies this transfer function directly to the DCT coefficients of the luminance channel:

\[\text{MSE}_{HVS} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{u} \sum_{v} \left( (C_{u,v}^{\text{orig}} - C_{u,v}^{\text{comp}}) \cdot A\left(\sqrt{u^2 + v^2}\right) \right)^2\]

Because \(A(f)\) rapidly decays at high spatial frequencies, this algorithm accurately isolates whether loss of high-frequency luminance will be imperceptible or cause visible blurriness.

Visual Information Fidelity (VIF) and Detail Loss Metric (DLM)

Visual Information Fidelity treats the compression process as a distortion channel within an information-theoretic framework. Implemented via Gaussian Scale Mixtures (GSM) in the steerable pyramid wavelet domain, VIF models the source luminance signal as an information transmitter. High-frequency loss is computed by evaluating the mutual information between the original subbands and the quantized subbands:

\[\text{VIF} = \frac{\sum_{k} I(C^k; F^k | s^k)}{\sum_{k} I(C^k; E^k | s^k)}\]

Where \(I\) denotes mutual information, \(C^k\) represents original coefficients in subband \(k\), \(E^k\) represents the perceived original signal, and \(F^k\) is the decoded signal corrupted by quantization noise and channel attenuation.

A subset metric specifically used in video analysis is the Detail Loss Metric (DLM). DLM separates global impairment (such as color shifts or broad luminance offsets) from high-frequency structural dropouts by evaluating edge preservation along high-contrast boundaries.

Multi-Scale Structural Similarity (MS-SSIM)

While basic SSIM measures luminance, contrast, and structure at a single resolution, Multi-Scale SSIM (MS-SSIM) iteratively downsamples the luminance channel over \(M\) scales using a low-pass filter and decimation:

\[\text{MS-SSIM}(x, y) = [l_M(x, y)]^{\alpha_M} \cdot \prod_{j=1}^{M} [c_j(x, y)]^{\beta_j} [s_j(x, y)]^{\gamma_j}\]

  • \(l_M\) calculates baseline luminance comparison at the lowest resolution.
  • \(c_j\) and \(s_j\) measure contrast and structural correlation at scale \(j\).

High-frequency luminance loss manifests primarily at the finest scales (\(j = 1\) and \(j = 2\)). A drop in \(c_1\) and \(s_1\) with stable metrics at \(j \ge 3\) indicates that high-frequency textures are being stripped while low-frequency image structure remains intact.

Lagrangian Rate-Distortion Optimization (RDO)

Within WebM encoders like libvpx (VP9) and libaom (AV1), internal prediction of luminance degradation is calculated in real time using the Lagrangian cost function:

\[J = D + \lambda \cdot R\]

To account for spatial detail, encoders calculate distortion \(D\) using variance-adaptive sum of squared differences (SSD). The local spatial variance \(\sigma^2\) acts as an algorithm for estimating high-frequency content:

\[\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (Y_i - \mu)^2\]

In flat blocks (low \(\sigma^2\)), high-frequency luminance loss is immediately obvious as banding or ringing artifacts. In complex textures (high \(\sigma^2\)), masking effects permit the encoder to increase quantization without perceptible degradation. The mathematical relationship between \(\sigma^2\) and the chosen quantization step size \(\Delta(q)\) serves as the encoder's internal predictor for luminance fidelity.
