Opus Frequency Splitting for Voice and Music

The Opus audio codec, standard in WebM media containers, achieves high-fidelity reproduction of both speech and music through a hybrid architecture that splits audio into distinct frequency bands. By dynamically dividing the spectrum, Opus routes lower frequencies to a voice-optimized Linear Predictive Coding (LPC) engine and upper frequencies to a transform-based music engine. This article details the specific frequency-splitting mechanisms, filter bank architectures, and band-blending techniques Opus utilizes to merge voice and music seamlessly into a single track.

The Hybrid Architecture: SILK and CELT

Opus combines two independent audio coding algorithms: SILK and CELT. SILK, originally created for Skype, is optimized for human voice through LPC, excelling at low bitrates and lower frequencies. CELT (Constrained Energy Lapped Transform) relies on the Modified Discrete Cosine Transform (MDCT), making it ideal for music, ambient noise, and complex high-frequency harmonics.

To blend these two types of signals, Opus operates in three operational states:

  • SILK-only mode: Used for pure speech at lower bitrates and narrow bandwidths.
  • CELT-only mode: Used for dedicated music, low-latency applications, or fullband transients.
  • Hybrid mode: Employs frequency splitting to run both encoders simultaneously on different portions of the spectrum.

The 8 kHz Crossover Point

In Hybrid mode, Opus processes Superwideband (12 kHz audio bandwidth / 24 kHz sampling rate) or Fullband (20 kHz audio bandwidth / 48 kHz sampling rate) audio by splitting the input spectrum at an 8 kHz boundary:

  1. The Low Band (0 Hz to 8 kHz): Encoded with SILK. This range covers the fundamental frequencies and the most critical formants of human speech.
  2. The High Band (8 kHz to 20 kHz): Encoded with CELT. This range handles the breathiness of voice, harmonic air, percussion transients, and instrumental overtones.

Because human ear sensitivity to pitch precision drops in higher registers, CELT encodes the high band using energy preservation rather than precise waveform matching, saving significant bitrate while preserving perceptual brightness.

Complementary Filter Banks and QMF Implementation

To split the frequency bands without introducing phase distortion, Opus employs a two-band critically sampled filter bank structure based on Quadrature Mirror Filters (QMF):

  • Low-Pass / High-Pass Complementary Filtering: The input audio stream passes through a pair of linear-phase finite impulse response (FIR) filters. The low-pass branch downsamples and isolates the speech baseband, while the high-pass branch isolates the top band.
  • Phase Alignment and Delay Matching: Linear predictive synthesis inherently introduces algorithmic delays different from overlap-add transform windows. Opus introduces fixed-delay compensation buffers in the CELT path to match the algorithmic lookahead and processing delay of the SILK path. This alignment guarantees that the transient edges of the high and low bands arrive simultaneously at the decoder.
  • Critically Sampled Decimation: The 0–8 kHz band is decimated to a 16 kHz sample rate suitable for SILK's internal pipeline, while CELT natively computes MDCT frames matching the remaining sampling spectrum. This eliminates computational redundancy by processing only the necessary samples per domain.

Spectral Recombination and Artifact Suppression

At the decoding phase inside the WebM container pipeline, the two bands are decoded separately and synthesized back into a unified signal:

  • Overlap-Add Smoothing: The reconstructed CELT high band and SILK low band are upsampled back to the master output rate (typically 48 kHz).
  • Complementary Synthesis Filters: The bands pass through synthesis QMF stages that mirror the analysis filters. The complementary frequency responses cancel out adjacent aliasing effects that occur near the 8 kHz crossover zone.
  • Dynamic Spectral Tilt Matching: Because speech codecs and transform codecs model audio energy profiles differently, Opus applies dynamic gain adjustments near the boundary. This normalization prevents an audible "shelf" or spectral gap between the upper limit of the voice channel and the lower limit of the music channel.

By coupling a critically sampled QMF network with dedicated delay compensation, Opus enables WebM audio tracks to maintain intelligible voice formants and crisp musical acoustics within a single unified stream.
