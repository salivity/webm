CQ vs CRF in WebM Video Encoding

When encoding WebM videos using codecs like VP9 or AV1, choosing between Constant Quantization (CQ) and Constant Rate Factor (CRF) determines how data is distributed across frames. While both approaches aim to control video quality rather than strictly targeting a specific file size, they handle visual complexity differently. This article breaks down the fundamental mechanics, visual differences, compression efficiencies, and practical trade-offs between CQ and CRF in WebM encoding.

Understanding the Difference

Constant Quantization (CQ)

Constant Quantization applies a fixed mathematical compression parameter to every macroblock or frame in the video stream. It treats all visual data equally, applying the exact same level of quantization regardless of whether a scene is a static talking head or a fast-paced action sequence.

Constant Rate Factor (CRF)

Constant Rate Factor (or Constrained Quality mode in VP9/AV1 terminology) adjusts quantization dynamically based on human visual perception. It raises compression on scenes with high motion or complex noise where compression artifacts are difficult for human eyes to detect, and lowers compression on smooth, static scenes where banding and blockiness are readily visible.

Key Trade-Offs

1. Perceptual Quality vs. Mathematical Uniformity

  • CRF: Delivers consistent visual quality from the viewer's perspective. The perceived clarity remains stable across different types of scenes throughout the video.
  • CQ: Delivers consistent mathematical compression, which leads to inconsistent perceptual quality. Simple scenes may show noticeable artifacts, while complex scenes receive more precision than the human eye can discern.

2. File Size and Compression Efficiency

  • CRF: Highly efficient. By shedding data where it is visually imperceptible and investing bits where detail is critical, CRF achieves smaller file sizes for the same perceived visual quality compared to CQ.
  • CQ: Inefficient. CQ often wastes significant bandwidth on noisy, high-motion scenes without offering a perceptible visual improvement, leading to bloated file sizes.

3. Bitrate Predictability and Capping

  • CRF with Bitrate Bounds: In WebM encoders such as libvpx-vp9, CRF can be paired with an upper bitrate limit (-b:v combined with -crf). This creates a "constrained quality" mode ideal for web delivery, ensuring files remain under streaming bandwidth limits while maintaining variable-scene efficiency.
  • CQ: Pure CQ provides no direct mechanism to limit bitrate spikes during intense scenes, making it risky for network streaming or bandwidth-capped environments.

4. Encoding Overhead

  • CRF: Requires slightly more computational overhead to analyze scene complexity and adjust quantization matrices dynamically.
  • CQ: Bypasses psycho-visual rate-control calculations, resulting in marginally faster encoding times, though modern encoder optimizations have made this performance gap negligible.

Practical Recommendations

  • Choose CRF for almost all production use cases, including web streaming, video archiving, and general WebM delivery. It offers the best balance of visual fidelity and bandwidth efficiency.
  • Choose CQ primarily for algorithmic benchmarking, codec development, or academic testing where raw, unadjusted mathematical compression consistency is required across test sequences.
