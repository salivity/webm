Why WebM Excludes Non-Open Audio Codecs

The decision to exclude proprietary audio codecs from the WebM specification was driven by the project's foundational mission: to create a completely open, royalty-free media format for the web. Spearheaded by Google and supported by organizations like Mozilla and Opera, WebM was designed to deliver high-quality video and audio without the legal, financial, and technical constraints associated with proprietary formats like AAC and MP3. Key factors influencing this restriction included the avoidance of patent licensing fees, the elimination of legal liabilities for open-source developers, the pursuit of platform interoperability, and the presence of high-performing open alternatives like Vorbis and Opus.

Patent Licensing and Financial Burdens

The primary catalyst for restricting WebM to open audio formats was the complex patent landscape surrounding digital media. Proprietary codecs, such as AAC (Advanced Audio Coding), are governed by patent pools like Via Licensing, which require commercial entities and software distributors to pay significant royalties.

For free and open-source software (FOSS) projects—such as the Chromium browser, Mozilla Firefox, and Linux distributions—distributing patented codecs creates severe logistical and financial hurdles. These projects cannot legally bundle proprietary decoders without incurring massive licensing costs or compromising their open distribution licenses. By mandating open codecs, WebM ensured that developers could build, distribute, and modify playback software without negotiating patent licenses or paying per-unit fees.

Promoting Open Web Standards

The introduction of the HTML5 <video> and <audio> elements was intended to make media playback a native browser capability, eliminating the need for third-party plugins like Adobe Flash. However, the early HTML5 landscape fractured because commercial browser vendors favored proprietary standards (H.264/AAC), while open-source advocates favored unencumbered formats.

Integrating non-open audio codecs into WebM would have defeated its purpose as an alternative standard. Allowing proprietary audio inside an open container would reintroduce the dependency on closed-source technologies, undermining the principle that the open web should be built on publicly documented, unencumbered protocols accessible to all participants equally.

Legal Protection for Content Creators and Distributors

Proprietary codecs often carry ambiguous licensing terms that can extend beyond software vendors to content distributors. Streaming platforms, independent webmasters, and content creators risked potential patent litigation or future royalty demands simply for hosting audio encoded with proprietary algorithms.

By strictly limiting the specification to codecs verified to be open and royalty-free, the WebM project created a legally safe pipeline. Creators could encode, host, and distribute media without fear of retroactive licensing claims from patent holders.

Technical Viability of Open Alternatives

A practical factor in excluding non-open codecs was that high-quality, open-source alternatives already existed. WebM initially launched with support for Vorbis, a mature, patent-free audio codec that offered fidelity comparable to MP3 and AAC at similar bitrates.

Later, the specification expanded to include Opus, developed by the Xiph.Org Foundation, Mozilla, and Skype (IETF RFC 6716). Opus surpassed both Vorbis and proprietary alternatives like AAC-LC in performance, latency, and versatility. Because an open audio codec existed that technically matched or exceeded proprietary standards, there was no engineering justification to compromise the format's open philosophy.
