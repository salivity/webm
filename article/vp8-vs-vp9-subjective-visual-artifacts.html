VP8 vs VP9: Subjective Visual Artifacts

WebM VP8 and VP9 are both open-source video codecs developed by Google, but their underlying architectures handle bandwidth constraints and data loss in distinctly different ways. When subjected to low bitrates, VP8 and VP9 exhibit unique visual degradation patterns. This guide examines the primary subjective visual artifacts—such as macroblocking, edge ringing, blurring, color banding, and temporal flickering—that allow observers to visually differentiate VP8 video compression from VP9.

Macroblocking and Block Boundaries

The most apparent visual distinction between the two codecs lies in how block boundaries appear during compression:

  • VP8 (Rigid Grid Blockiness): VP8 relies on a fixed 16x16 pixel macroblock structure. When compressed heavily, it produces a rigid, uniform grid pattern across the frame. Complex textures and high-motion areas quickly disintegrate into visible square tiles with sharp, defined edges.
  • VP9 (Variable-Size Partitioning): VP9 uses a hierarchical superblock structure ranging from 64x64 down to 4x4 pixels. Under heavy compression, VP9 rarely produces uniform grid lines. Instead, artifacts manifest as non-uniform patches of varying sizes, often appearing as larger smoothed regions rather than a distinct checkerboard of squares.

Edge Ringing and Mosquito Noise

Artifacts around sharp, high-contrast edges (such as text, silhouettes, or subtitles) differ noticeably between the two formats:

  • VP8: VP8 frequently exhibits coarse "mosquito noise"—a swarm of fluctuating, high-frequency artifacts around sharp outlines—along with pronounced ringing halos. This is caused by VP8's limited transform sizes (typically 4x4) and less adaptive deblocking filters.
  • VP9: VP9 features transform sizes up to 32x32 and a more sophisticated in-loop deblocking filter. Consequently, edges remain cleaner. When edge degradation occurs in VP9, it appears as soft smearing or localized fading rather than high-frequency noise clusters.

Texture Retention vs. Aggressive Smoothing

How each codec sacrifices spatial detail under low-bitrate pressure affects the texture of human skin, grass, and background surfaces:

  • VP8 (Pixelated Detail Loss): VP8 tends to preserve a degree of surface grain, but as the bitrate drops, this grain collapses into jagged, pixelated noise. Textures degrade into noisy, noisy patterns before losing all form.
  • VP9 (Waxy/Plastic Smoothing): VP9 prioritizes structural outlines and overall geometry over high-frequency texture. At constrained bitrates, VP9 applies strong internal smoothing, resulting in a characteristic "waxy" or "airbrushed" appearance on surfaces such as skin, fabric, and foliage.

Color Banding and Gradient Posterization

Smooth color transitions, such as sunrises, skies, and dark scenes, highlight the mathematical differences in how the codecs process gradients:

  • VP8: Limited exclusively to an 8-bit color depth and smaller transform blocks, VP8 exhibits severe color banding (posterization) in low-contrast gradients. Stepping between color shades appears harsh and fragmented.
  • VP9: VP9's larger block partitions allow gradients to remain visually continuous over larger spatial areas. Furthermore, VP9 supports 10-bit and 12-bit color profiles (Profiles 2 and 3), drastically reducing or eliminating banding artifacts in gradient-heavy content.

Temporal Stability and "Pulsing"

Temporal artifacts refer to visual inconsistencies that occur from frame to frame over time:

  • VP8 (Keyframe Pumping): VP8 often suffers from noticeable "breathing" or "pumping" artifacts. Viewers can perceive a visible jump in clarity at each keyframe (I-frame), followed by progressive degradation across subsequent inter-frames (P-frames).
  • VP9 (Compound Prediction Stability): VP9 incorporates enhanced reference frame buffers and compound prediction methods. Motion remains visually coherent over time, and inter-frame transitions are substantially more stable, minimizing noticeable pulsing cycles between keyframes.
